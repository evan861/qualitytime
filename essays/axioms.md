# Axioms

*Finding the still points inside the exponential turning*

The Axial Age has been on my mind a lot lately. I've been thinking about the nature of the shift we're inside of right now — the sense that we're not just in a faster version of the world we knew, but that something deeper has pivoted. The Axial Age keeps feeling like the right historical echo: a moment when human consciousness didn't just learn new things, but changed the vista of its perception.

Seemingly separately, I'd also been thinking about axioms. I was recently reading about the battle between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry — how a single disputed assumption about parallel lines eventually cracked open into two entirely different shaped systems of reality, both valid, both coherent, just built on different starting points.

I don't know why, but this morning I finally noticed those two words — axial, axiom — sounded so similar. So I did what I like to do. I went on a little word journey.

Why don't you come along with me — if my assumptions are correct, I think you'll enjoy it.

The oldest traceable ancestor of both words is the Proto-Indo-European *aks-* — meaning **axle**. Not the wheel. Not the motion. The rod through the center of the wheel. The still thing around which turning becomes possible.

Hold that image. We'll need it.

**Axis.** Latin for axle, pivot line. In geometry, the reference line from which all positions are measured. It doesn't appear in the data. Everything else is located in relation to it.

**Axle.** The physical version. Iron. Literal. The wheel spins; the axle holds.

**Axiom.** From Greek *axioma* — "that which is thought worthy." Worthy of being a starting point. Worthy of organizing everything else around.

Here's where it gets interesting. Axiom may branch from a slightly different root than axis — *ag-*, meaning to drive, to weigh, to lead. But listen to what the two roots share structurally:

> An axle is what rotation organizes around.
> An axiom is what reasoning organizes around.

Same role. Different material. The axiom is the logical axle. The still point inside the turning.

**Axiology.** The study of value and worth. Same *axios* root. The branch of philosophy that asks: what is worthy of being a starting point? Which is, I postulate, one of the most important questions there is.

**The Axial Age.**

Karl Jaspers coined this in 1949. *Achsenzeit* in German: axis-time. His claim: roughly 800–200 BCE, something pivoted in human consciousness. Buddha, Confucius, Lao Tzu, Socrates, the Hebrew prophets, Zoroaster — none knew each other, all arrived near-simultaneously across disconnected civilizations. Human thought turned on something.

He called it the axis because he saw it as the hinge point of world history. Everything before and after is located in relation to it.

What they all did, in their different ways, was step back from the motion and ask: what is the thing everything is rotating around? Is it the right axis? Could there be another?

## The pattern

The axis is never the most visible thing. It's the condition for visible motion. The wheel is dramatic. The axle is quiet. You don't notice the axle when the wheels are turning fine.

Axioms work exactly this way. They are invisible precisely because everything else is moving in relation to them. They stop feeling like choices — unprovable starting points we agreed were coherent enough to use — and start feeling like the shape of reality itself.

Until the wheel changes.

## A story about parallel lines

For roughly two thousand years, mathematicians tried to prove Euclid's fifth postulate. You probably know it intuitively: through any point not on a given line, there is exactly one line that runs parallel to it. Parallel lines never meet.

It seemed too obvious to be an axiom — too complex to be a true starting point. Surely it could be derived from simpler principles. Generation after generation tried. All failed.

Then, in the early 19th century, a few mathematicians — Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Gauss — did something different. They stopped trying to prove it and asked: what if it's simply not true? What if, through that same point, you could draw multiple parallel lines? What if parallel lines could curve away from each other indefinitely?

The response from parts of the mathematical establishment was something close to scandal. This felt like heresy. Gauss, who had independently arrived at the same conclusions earlier, reportedly feared ridicule and held back from publishing. The old geometry felt like reality itself, not a choice.

But the new geometries held. They were internally consistent. No contradictions. Just different shapes of space — curved, hyperbolic, strange to the intuition but mathematically airtight.

Then Riemann asked the opposite question: what if parallel lines always meet? What if there are no true parallels at all? Draw two lines that appear parallel on a globe and extend them — they converge at the poles.

Three geometries. Three different answers to the same axiom about parallel lines. All valid. All coherent. All useful — in different domains. Einstein would later show that actual spacetime is non-Euclidean; the universe we live in curves.

The battle wasn't over which geometry was right. It was over whether we were allowed to notice that geometry had been resting on a choice. An axiom. A starting point so embedded it had become invisible — had become, in most people's minds, the shape of reality itself.

**Change the axiom. Change the shape of everything.**

We are not arriving at an axial moment. We are already inside one — and the disorientation many people feel, the ambient sense that the old maps are slightly off, may be exactly what it feels like to live between axioms.

The old ones haven't fully dissolved. The new ones haven't been agreed upon. We are in the gap.

This is different from saying we have better information or faster tools, though we do. Some of the starting points — the unprovable but agreed-upon assumptions that everything else is built on — have already shifted, or been shattered outright, without most people noticing.

In 2022, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for experiments confirming that reality is fundamentally non-local. Particles remain correlated across any distance, instantly — not because a signal travels between them, but because locality itself, the assumption that things only affect adjacent things, turns out to have been an axiom, not a law. The universe doesn't work the way our inherited intuitions assume.

That finding was published. Celebrated. And then largely set aside — because updating an axiom is not like updating a fact. You can learn a new fact in an afternoon. Updating an axiom means reorganizing the entire geometry that rests on it. Most of us are still running on the old map. Not out of ignorance. But because the new one hasn't been drawn yet in a form we can live inside.

That is the between-axioms condition. The shapes are changing. And we are navigating the transition mostly without instruments calibrated for where we actually are.

The Axial Age was the historical moment when humanity — across cultures with no contact — simultaneously noticed the axle. Stepped back far enough to see that the still point was a choice, not a given.

But how? What made that stepping-back possible across so many civilizations at once?

One answer is writing. Not writing as storage — writing as the first technology that made thought inspectable. Before writing, thought was ephemeral. It happened and dissolved. Once words could exist outside the mind on a surface, a person could revisit an idea, compare it to another idea, find the contradiction, stabilize the abstraction. Thought became cumulative across generations. More importantly: the mind could watch itself think.

That is not an incremental improvement. That is a phase transition in the nature of consciousness itself.

Writing didn't give humans new facts. It gave them a new apparatus for thinking — the way a telescope isn't just a tool for seeing farther, it's a device that changes the quality of seeing itself. The Axial sages weren't smarter than their ancestors. They had a better mirror.

We are being asked to do that again.

Not to discard the old axioms — Euclidean geometry is not wrong, it just has a specific shape, valid in its domain. But to *see them as axioms*. Chosen. Unprovable. Enormously generative within their shape. And no longer sufficient as the only map.

The first move is the hardest: to see the axle you've been riding on as an axle at all.

Gödel, it seems, would agree: you cannot use your own axioms to verify your own axioms.

There is an irony worth sitting with in the current wave of people building "second brains" — knowledge graphs in tools like Obsidian, Roam, Notion. The stated goal is usually to capture ideas, make connections, think better. But something else happens when you graph your knowledge over time: the axioms start to surface. Not labeled as axioms. Just as the nodes everything keeps linking back to. The starting points you never questioned because you were too busy building from them. The second brain, if you look at it long enough, shows you the shape of the first one — and the axles it's been running on.

Did everyone think they were just building productivity tools — while collectively assembling the proverbial cognitive layer of the stack?

At the very least, everyone will start to see a knowledge graph of what they resonate with.

Maybe that's a good start.

The still point is always there. The question is whether we've noticed it.
